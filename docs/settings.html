    D.2How to read the measurements

    Each card below says what a setting does, what values the search tries, and how it moved accuracy. The numbers come from a balanced comparison: 96 networks, each trained for the full 80 epochs and scored on the chip simulator, in which every option appeared equally often next to random shapes. A bar is the average deployed validation accuracy of the networks that used that value.

    What counts as a real difference

    Scores across those 96 networks spread by about 13 points, so with about 48 networks per option, a difference smaller than about 5 points could easily be luck. The tags say how much to believe each one: large is far outside the noise, moderate is probably real, and unclear is within the noise. Every result is for 80 epochs of training; some settings, especially the ones that fight overfitting, may behave differently in longer runs.

    D.6Normalization

    Keeping each layer's activity in a healthy range during training. Both options fold away completely at deployment.

    D.7Regularization

    Settings that make memorising the training people harder, to close the gap between training and validation accuracy (page B). They tend to slow learning down, so a short run can undersell them.

    D.9What mattered most

    Every two-way or three-way setting from the balanced comparison, ranked by the gap between its best and worst option.

    In short

    For deployed accuracy after 80 epochs, the head matters most: flatten beats GAP by about 13 points. Most other settings move accuracy by a few points at most, and many are within noise. The best networks share long clips (T = 48), one bit per pixel, signed spikes, a flatten head, a shrunk first layer and thresholds that fold the bias in, but the largest remaining problem is overfitting, which no single setting here fixes.
