The small grid of weights a convolution slides across the picture, for example 5 × 5. A.4

One of several stacked pictures. The camera gives two (brighter and darker events), and each layer makes one per kernel. A.4

How far a kernel moves at each step. A stride of 2 roughly halves the size of the map. A.4
pooling
Shrinking a map by replacing each 2 × 2 block with one number: its average (average pool) or its largest value (max pool). A.4
head
The last part of the network, which turns the feature maps into one score per class. A.4
leak (τ)
How fast a spiking neuron's potential fades, set by τ (tau): each step it loses 1/τ of itself. On the chip τ must be a power of two. A.5
threshold (θ)
The potential a spiking neuron must exceed to fire, written θ (theta). On the chip it lies between 0 and 1. A.5
signed spikes
Spikes that can be +1 or −1. On the chip each signed unit is two neurons, one for each sign. A.11
trial
One candidate network the search builds, trains and scores. C.1
balanced block
The first 96 trials of a search, designed so every option appears equally often, and never stopped early. C.7

    D.3How to read the measurements

    Forgot some of these?balanced block · validation set · percentage point · simulator
    balanced block
    The first 96 trials of a search, designed so every option appears equally often, and never stopped early. C.7
    validation set
    Examples held back from training and used to choose between networks: here 216 clips from 4 people. B.3
    percentage point
    The difference between two percentages: going from 74% to 76% is 2 percentage points. B.5
    simulator
    A program that computes exactly what the chip would. Every trained network is checked on one, clip by clip. C.4

    Each card below says what a setting does, its name in the search records, what values the search tries, and how it moved accuracy. The measurements come from the DVS128 Gesture search described on page C: its first 96 trials were a balanced comparison, 96 networks each trained for the full 80 epochs and scored on the chip simulator, with every option appearing equally often next to random shapes. A bar is the average deployed validation accuracy of the networks that used that value.

    What counts as a real difference

    Scores across those 96 networks spread by about 13 points, so with about 48 networks per option, a difference smaller than about 5 points could easily be luck. The tags say how much to believe each one: large is far outside the noise, moderate is probably real, and unclear is within the noise. Every result is for 80 epochs of training; some settings, especially the ones that fight overfitting, may behave differently in longer runs.

    D.6The neuron

    Forgot some of these?leak · threshold · signed spikes · bias neuron · folding
    leak (τ)
    How fast a spiking neuron's potential fades, set by τ (tau): each step it loses 1/τ of itself. On the chip τ must be a power of two. A.5
    threshold (θ)
    The potential a spiking neuron must exceed to fire, written θ (theta). On the chip it lies between 0 and 1. A.5
    signed spikes
    Spikes that can be +1 or −1. On the chip each signed unit is two neurons, one for each sign. A.11
    bias neuron
    A chip neuron that fires on every step. Its connections deliver a constant bias to the neurons it feeds, at the cost of one connection each. G.5
    folding
    Merging one operation into another so the chip does not have to run it: batch normalization into the weights and thresholds, or an average pool into the next layer's connections. G.5

    How the spiking neurons behave. All of these deploy exactly; the chip stores a leak, a threshold and a sign for every neuron.

    D.7Normalization

    Keeping each layer's activity in a healthy range during training. Both options fold away completely at deployment.

    D.8Regularization

    Forgot some of these?overfitting · dropout · weight decay · label smoothing · augmentation
    overfitting
    Learning the training examples too well, including details that do not carry over to new ones. It shows as a gap between training and validation accuracy. B.4
    dropout
    Silencing a random set of units during training, so the network cannot rely on any single one. D.8
    weight decay
    A small pull of every weight toward zero on each step, which discourages memorizing. C.2
    label smoothing
    Training toward a target that gives the right answer slightly less than 100%, which discourages overconfidence. D.8
    augmentation
    Making new training examples by changing old ones in ways that keep their label, such as shifting a clip a few pixels. B.6

    Settings that make memorizing the training people harder, to close the gap between training and validation accuracy (page B). They tend to slow learning down, so a short run can undersell them.

    D.9Optimization

    Forgot some of these?learning rate · loss · optimizer · learning-rate schedule · TET · early stopping
    learning rate
    How big each training step is. Too small wastes time; too large overshoots and bounces around. A.3
    loss (cross-entropy)
    One number that says how wrong the network's answers were; training tries to make it smaller. Cross-entropy is the usual loss when the answer is one of several classes. A.3
    optimizer (Adam, AdamW)
    The rule that turns gradients into weight changes. Adam and AdamW give each weight its own step size based on its recent gradients. C.2
    learning-rate schedule
    A rule for changing the learning rate over a run, usually from larger to smaller, for example smoothly (cosine) or in stages (step). D.9
    TET
    Temporal efficient training: a loss that scores the output at every step instead of only the total over the clip. C.2
    early stopping
    Ending a trial before its full training run because it is clearly behind the others. C.9

    How the weights are updated and what the loss rewards. The learning rate does not have to stay fixed: a schedule changes it over the run, usually starting larger and ending smaller, so early steps cover ground and late steps settle in.

    INTERACTIVE Learning-rate schedules The four schedules a run can use. Warm-up, when set, ramps up from 10% of the base rate over the first epochs.

    Cosine and one-cycle deliver most of their benefit late in the run, which is why early stopping waits until epoch 30 before judging a trial (page C).

    D.10What mattered most

    Every two-way or three-way setting from the balanced comparison, ranked by the gap between its best and worst option.

    In short

    For deployed accuracy after 80 epochs, the head matters most: flatten beats GAP by about 13 points. Most other settings move accuracy by a few points at most, and many are within noise. The twelve best networks the search has trained so far share long clips (T = 48), one bit per pixel, signed spikes, a flatten head, a shrunk first layer and thresholds that fold the bias in, but the largest remaining problem is overfitting, which no single setting here fixes.
