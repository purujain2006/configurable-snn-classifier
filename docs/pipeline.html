The whole pipeline, and the search

From a gesture in front of a camera to a network running on the chip: every stage a candidate network goes through, and the search that decides which candidates to try and which one wins. Step through it in one picture first, then read each stage in detail.

C.1The pipeline in one picture

Why this exists

Hundreds of candidates are compared, so every one has to be built, trained and judged the same way, or the comparison means nothing. Training also takes a long time on expensive GPUs, so a candidate that cannot win should be found out as cheaply as possible.

The search tries a few hundred candidate networks. Each candidate is a trial, and every trial goes through the same stages. Cheap stages come first, so a candidate that cannot win is dropped before it costs much.

C.2One training step

Training repeats one step thousands of times. A batch of 16 clips runs through the network for T timesteps plus the flush steps, the output spikes are counted, the counts are compared with the right answers, and every weight moves a little in the direction that would have made the answers better.

  1. Forward. The batch runs through the network step by step, giving one spike count per class for each clip. The neurons' potentials are then reset, so one clip cannot affect the next.
  2. Loss. The counts are turned into a loss, one number that says how wrong the answers were (page A).
  3. Backward. Gradients flow back through every step, through each spike by the surrogate gradient (page A) and through each rounded weight by the straight-through estimator (page G).
  4. Update. Very large gradients can be capped first (gradient clipping), then the optimizer moves every weight one small step.

There are two ways to compute the loss, and the search tries both. The rate loss scores the spike counts over the whole clip, which is exactly the chip's answer. TET (temporal efficient training) scores the output at every timestep and averages, so every step is pushed to be right on its own, which can make the counts more reliable. Either way, the answer is read from the counts.

The optimizer is Adam or AdamW, two common rules that give every weight its own step size based on its recent gradients. Weight decay, a pull of every weight toward zero that discourages memorizing, applies only to connection weights. Applied to the leak τ or the threshold θ, it would quietly change how the neurons behave on the chip.

C.3Two training phases

The chip stores every weight as a 16-bit integer, so a deployed weight can only take one of 65,535 evenly spaced values between −1 and 1. A network trained with ordinary numbers and rounded at the end can lose a lot of accuracy, because training quietly relied on precise values that rounding destroys. So training happens in two phases. The first quarter uses ordinary weights. Then the weights are snapped to the integer grid, and the rest of training happens on the grid, so the network learns to work with exactly the weights it will deploy with.

ANIMATION Weights during training, before and after the switch Each dot is one weight. The vertical lines are the values the chip can store; the spacing here is exaggerated thousands of times so it is visible.

Before the switch the weights drift freely. After it, every weight sits exactly on a storable value, and a weight can only move by jumping to a neighboring one. What the network computes from here on is exactly what the chip will compute.

At the switch, batch normalization (a training aid that rescales each channel) is also folded into the weights and thresholds, because the chip has no such operation. Folding needs the averages batch normalization collected during the first phase, which is why it happens at the switch and not at the start. Page G shows the fold and what it costs.

PhaseWhat happens
warm-upThe first quarter of the epochs train with ordinary weights and batch normalization, so its averages settle.
on the gridThe best warm-up weights are reloaded, batch normalization is folded in, and the remaining epochs train on integer weights at half the learning rate.
exportThe network is frozen into the chip's format and scored on validation.
simulator checkThe validation clips run through the chip simulator, and every answer is compared with the trained network's (C.4).

After each epoch the network is scored on validation. With only 216 validation clips, one epoch's score can swing by a couple of points by chance, so picking the single best epoch tends to pick a lucky one. The tool keeps the weights from the end of the best five-epoch stretch instead, judged by its average.

Other schedules for the switch

Switching a quarter of the way through is the default. The tool can also train entirely with ordinary weights and round once at the end, which shows how much conversion costs a network that never saw it, or round at the end and then train a few more epochs as a repair. Both lose more accuracy than training most of the run on the grid.

C.4Export, and the simulator check

Why this exists

The training software and the chip describe a network differently. The software thinks in layers of decimal numbers; the chip thinks in single neurons, connections and whole numbers. Export translates one into the other. The simulator exists because that translation has to be exact: if the chip and the trained network disagree on even a few clips, the accuracy measured after training is not the accuracy the chip delivers.

Forgot some of these?membrane potential · leak · threshold · INT16 · export · simulator · FPGA
membrane potential
The one number a spiking neuron keeps. It rises with input, leaks over time, and the neuron fires when it passes the threshold. A.5
leak (τ)
How fast a spiking neuron's potential fades, set by τ (tau): each step it loses 1/τ of itself. On the chip τ must be a power of two. A.5
threshold (θ)
The potential a spiking neuron must exceed to fire, written θ (theta). On the chip it lies between 0 and 1. A.5
INT16 (16-bit integer)
A whole number stored in 16 bits, from −32,767 to 32,767. Every weight and threshold on the chip is one, with 32,767 standing for 1.0. G.4
export
Writing a trained network out in the chip's own format: tables of neurons, connections, integer weights and thresholds. C.4
simulator
A program that computes exactly what the chip would. Every trained network is checked on one, clip by clip. C.4
FPGA
A chip whose circuits can be reprogrammed after it is made. HiAER-Spike is built from them. Home

After training, each layer is written out in the chip's own format: tables of neurons, the connections between them, integer weights, integer thresholds and leak settings. Then every validation clip runs through a simulator that uses the chip's exact integer arithmetic, and its answer on each clip is compared with the trained network's. A network whose numbers do not fit the chip, or that the simulator cannot run, is marked as not deployable and scores zero; any clip where the two answers differ is counted and reported.

The reference is the simulator the HiAER-Spike team ships with the chip's software. It is the target, rather than the physical chip, because according to that team's own tests the current FPGA build handles the leak of negative potentials incorrectly. The reference simulator stores every connection as a separate object, so a large network needs about 150 GB of memory, and a long wait, just to load before it simulates a single clip. That cost motivates a second, fast simulator inside the tool. It does the reference simulator's exact arithmetic, step by step, but keeps each convolution as a convolution, and it checks all the validation clips of a trial in seconds.

The fast simulator is trusted only because it is tested. A self-test builds 18 small networks covering every layer type the tool deploys, runs each through both simulators, and compares every neuron's potential and spike at every step. All 18 match exactly, and the test also catches deliberate one-unit errors in the threshold, the bias and the leak.

In the search recordssim_disagreementsdeployable

Every trial's record says how this check went. For the example network, sim_disagreements = 0: on all 216 validation clips, the chip simulator and the trained network gave the same answer. deployable = True means the network fits the chip and the simulator ran it, so its score counts. Export itself never changes an answer, because training already used the chip's whole-number weights and its exact neuron (C.3).

The trial's score is the deployed network's accuracy on the validation people. Scoring the deployed network, rather than the network as it was during training, means the search is optimizing exactly what would run on the chip.

C.5What the search is for

A network here is described by about thirty settings (often called hyperparameters): how deep it is, how wide each layer is, how each layer shrinks the map, how the input is encoded, how the neurons behave and how training runs. Each setting changes accuracy, many change how much the others matter, and some combinations do not fit the chip at all. There are trillions of possible shapes before a single training setting is chosen, and every trial is a full training run on a GPU. The search exists to find a very good combination within a budget of a few hundred trials.

The search uses five ideas, each covered below: check the shape before anything else, start with a balanced comparison of every option, learn from finished trials where to look next, stop weak trials early, and retrain the best few before naming a winner.

C.6Shape first, then the chip check

A trial chooses its shape first: depth, widths, kernels, input size, bits, head and the options that change connection counts. The chip check runs on the shape alone. If it fails, the trial ends with a score of zero before any training setting is chosen.

Some settings exist only for some networks: a four-layer network has a kernel size for its fourth layer, and a two-layer network does not. Optuna builds each trial's settings step by step, so a trial only has the settings it actually uses. Change a choice below and watch which settings appear.

C.7Starting with a balanced comparison

Forgot some of these?pooling · bias neuron · flatten · GAP · signed spikes · bit · leak · TET · augmentation · dropout · firing rate
pooling
Shrinking a map by replacing each 2 × 2 block with one number: its average (average pool) or its largest value (max pool). A.4
bias neuron
A chip neuron that fires on every step. Its connections deliver a constant bias to the neurons it feeds, at the cost of one connection each. G.5
flatten
Laying every cell of every map out in one line, so the classifier knows where each feature appeared. A.4
GAP (global average pooling)
Averaging each map down to one number, so the classifier knows how much of each feature there was but not where. A.4
signed spikes
Spikes that can be +1 or −1. On the chip each signed unit is two neurons, one for each sign. A.11
bit
A binary digit, 0 or 1. With B bits a number can go from 0 to 2^B − 1: 1 bit gives 0 or 1, 4 bits give 0 to 15. E.1
leak (τ)
How fast a spiking neuron's potential fades, set by τ (tau): each step it loses 1/τ of itself. On the chip τ must be a power of two. A.5
TET
Temporal efficient training: a loss that scores the output at every step instead of only the total over the clip. C.2
augmentation
Making new training examples by changing old ones in ways that keep their label, such as shifting a clip a few pixels. B.6
dropout
Silencing a random set of units during training, so the network cannot rely on any single one. D.8
firing rate
How often neurons fire. A firing-rate penalty adds it to the loss, so the network learns to fire less and do less work. D.8

The first 96 trials are designed rather than sampled. They cover ten choices:

  • pooling: none, average or max
  • bias: in the threshold, or bias neurons
  • head: flatten or GAP
  • signed spikes: off or on
  • bits per pixel: one, or more than one
  • τ: fixed, learned per layer, or learned per channel
  • loss: rate or TET
  • augmentation, dropout between layers and the firing-rate penalty: each off or on

Every combination of all ten would take 2,304 trials. Instead every choice appears equally often across the 96, every pair of choices is spread as evenly as possible, and each trial gets a random shape that fits the chip. These trials all train for the full 80 epochs and are never stopped early.

There are two reasons. First, a fair comparison: if an option appeared mostly next to good shapes, it would look better than it is, and balancing removes that. Second, a fair start: the sampler learns from whatever it has seen, so an even first map of every option keeps it from locking onto whatever happened to be tried first. The full 80 epochs matter because some options, such as augmentation, learn slowly at first and would be cut unfairly by early stopping.

C.8Learning where to look

hyperparameter

After the designed trials, a sampler from Optuna proposes every new trial, using a method called the Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE). It splits finished trials into the best tenth and the rest, estimates for each setting where good trials tend to sit and where the rest sit, and proposes values that are common among good trials and rare among the rest. It looks at one setting at a time, but because "good" is judged on whole trials, values that work well in combination keep coming back together.

INTERACTIVE The sampler choosing a learning rate Dots are finished trials. Amber dots are the best tenth. The amber curve is where good trials sit and the grey curve where the rest sit; the dashed line is the next value the sampler will try, where the amber curve is highest relative to the grey one.

The hidden curve the trials are drawn from is not known to the sampler; each trial's score also has noise, as real training runs do. After a dozen trials the proposals crowd into the good region, while the occasional proposal elsewhere keeps checking that nothing better was missed.

C.9Stopping weak trials early

Forgot some of these?epoch · warm-up · integer grid
epoch
One pass through all of the training data. A trial here trains for up to 80 epochs. A.3
warm-up
The first stretch of a run. In training here, the first quarter of the epochs on ordinary weights; in a learning-rate schedule, a few epochs where the rate ramps up from small. C.3
integer grid
The evenly spaced values the chip can store. A weight on the grid is a whole number of steps of 1/32,767. G.4

Most trials will not win, and it is usually clear well before the end. At epoch 30, ten epochs after the switch to integer weights, each trial is compared with every other trial that has reached epoch 30, and only the best third continue to epoch 80. The rule is called ASHA, and two decisions shape it.

  • The checkpoint comes after the switch. Accuracy dips for a few epochs while a network adjusts to its integer weights. A checkpoint at or just after the switch would cut trials before they had trained on the weights they deploy with.
  • Trials in the balanced block are never stopped. Options such as augmentation and GAP heads learn slowly at first. The block exists to compare every option fairly, so every option there is judged on full runs.
ANIMATION Twelve trials meeting the checkpoint Each line is one trial's validation accuracy. Grey lines are trials from the designed block, which always run to the end. At epoch 30 the others are ranked, and the bottom two thirds stop.

How long to wait before the first checkpoint, the grace period, is a trade-off. Some learning-rate schedules improve most toward the end of training, so a grace period that is too short stops promising trials early. Try it: set the grace period to 1 and the late-improving curves are cut.

C.10Picking a winner fairly

Forgot some of these?validation set · test set · percentage point
validation set
Examples held back from training and used to choose between networks: here 216 clips from 4 people. B.3
test set
Examples kept aside until the very end and scored once: 288 clips from 6 people never seen in training. B.3
percentage point
The difference between two percentages: going from 74% to 76% is 2 percentage points. B.5

A trial's score depends partly on luck: the random starting weights and the order of the batches. Both come from a seed, a number that fixes every random choice of a run, so the same seed repeats a run exactly. With a validation set of 216 clips, two runs of the same configuration with different seeds can differ by several points (page B). So when the trials are done, the five best configurations are trained again from scratch with three new seeds each, and the winner is the configuration with the best average. Only then is its accuracy on the test people measured, once.

C.11A search, start to finish

A search can run for hours or days, depending on the GPUs available, so a mistake in its setup, such as a missing package, a dataset path that exists only on another machine, a trial too large for its share of the GPU, or a bug in the steps after the trials, would otherwise surface long after the search started. That motivates a preflight: before a search starts, every stage of it runs in miniature on the same machine. It checks the settings against each other, loads the data, measures the largest network on the GPU, trains two small networks and checks them against the simulator, and runs a six-trial search to the very end. The search refuses to start until a preflight has passed for the same settings and the same code.

An illustration of the shape of a search, not a record of one. The designed block takes the first stretch; after it, sampled trials score higher on average because the sampler concentrates on what worked, early stopping frees slots quickly, and rejected shapes cost no GPU time at all.

Every result is written to disk the moment a trial finishes, so a crash near the end loses nothing: one row per trial with its settings and scores, every trial's score after every epoch, the rejected shapes and the limits they broke, the best configuration so far and, at the end, each finalist's score for every seed. The search also ends early if a deployed network reaches the target accuracy, 97.5% for DVS128 Gesture, judged on the deployed score and never on a training-time one.

Five things stand out.

  • Every network in the block deployed. All 96 fit the chip, exported, and agreed with the simulator on every validation clip: zero disagreements across 20,736 clip checks.
  • The block's scores spread widely. The average is 53.2% and the middle score 55.8%, and about seven in ten scores fall within 13 points of the average. That spread is what makes the balanced comparison useful: differences between options show up against it (page D).
  • The sampler is learning. The 34 sampled trials that trained to the end average 68.9%, against 53.2% in the block, though none has yet beaten the block's best.
  • The sampler has settled on one design. Eleven of the twelve best trials share it: four layers, 40 × 40 frames, T = 48, one bit, signed spikes and tdBN, among other choices. Every one of the twelve uses T = 48, the largest value allowed.
  • The best network overfits. The example network scores 74.5% on validation but 99.7% on its own training clips (C.3). Closing that gap, more than fitting the chip, is what limits accuracy now.

The five best trials were then trained again for 300 epochs and measured on the test set. Validation rose by 2 to 8 points, and the four finished so far score 80.6% to 86.8% on test, all on networks that fit the chip. Page H covers those runs and what they mean.

In short

Every trial proposes a configuration, checks its shape against the chip, trains first with ordinary weights and then on the chip's integer grid, exports, and is scored by a simulator of the chip on held-out people. The search runs a balanced block of designed trials, then lets a sampler concentrate on what scores well, stops weak trials at epoch 30, and retrains the best five with fresh seeds before naming a winner. On DVS128 Gesture every trained network has deployed exactly, the sampler has converged on one design, and its best networks, trained longer, score 85 to 87% on test.
