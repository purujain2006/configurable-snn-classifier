Results

A search of 400 configurations selected five candidates for longer training. Their reported test accuracies range from 78.8% to 83.3% on DVS128 Gesture. The leading candidate uses more synaptic operations than the other four.

Finding

The leading candidate reports 83.33% test accuracy at 50.1 million synaptic operations (SynOps) per clip. A candidate at 80.56% uses 20.3 million. This comparison measures computational activity. Energy and variation across repeated training runs remain unmeasured.

Published reference: HiAER-Spike, DVS Gesture comparison reports 68.75% accuracy. Its models use different input resolutions and hardware measurements. The 14.6-point difference here is descriptive, not a matched reproduction or proof of equivalent hardware performance.

Evaluation protocol

Training data fits the weights. A validation set of 176 clips, sampled from the training partition, selects configurations and checkpoints. The test set contains 288 clips from six participants excluded from training.

Validation clips can share participants with training clips. Repeatedly selecting configurations against the same validation set can also favor those that happen to perform well on that set. Use the test scores for the external comparison, with the protocol limitations noted below.

The leading candidate reached 92.05% validation accuracy and 83.33% test accuracy. Each of the five retrained candidates was evaluated on test. Section 7.4 also reports test results for a weight-decay sweep, so the page does not describe a single test evaluation across the entire study.

Terms used in this page
Trial / epoch / checkpoint
A trial trains one sampled configuration. An epoch is one pass through the training data. A checkpoint saves model state or marks a scheduled comparison.
Validation / test
Validation selects settings and checkpoints. Test evaluates data held apart from training. Test results used to choose further experiments become part of development.
SynOps / dense MACs
SynOps count estimated spike-triggered additions. Dense MACs count multiplications followed by additions in a conventional network of the same shape. Neither directly measures energy.
Fan-in / fan-out
The number of incoming or outgoing connections at one neuron. The hardware limits these counts.
Kernel / channels / stride / pooling
A kernel is a filter’s spatial window. Channels are the output feature maps. Stride sets the spacing between filter positions. Pooling combines neighboring outputs to reduce spatial size.
Head / flatten / GAP
The head maps features to class scores. Flatten preserves spatial positions in a vector. Global average pooling (GAP) averages each channel over all spatial positions.
τ / θ
The leak constant τ controls how much membrane potential persists between steps. The threshold θ sets the potential required to emit a spike.
Learning rate / schedule / weight decay
The learning rate sets the optimizer’s update size. A learning-rate schedule changes it during training. Weight decay discourages large weights.
Dropout / label smoothing / gradient clipping
Dropout suppresses selected activations during training. Label smoothing distributes some target probability to other classes. Gradient clipping limits update-driving gradient magnitudes.
Quantization / folding
Quantization maps values to a finite numeric grid. Folding combines batch normalization with a neighboring layer for export. Export checks do not by themselves verify hardware inference.
Early stopping / pruning / scheduler
The search scheduler stops trials with low intermediate scores. This is distinct from the learning-rate schedule, which changes update sizes within a trial.
Random seed / uncertainty
A seed controls randomized initialization and training choices. Repeating a configuration across seeds estimates training variability. Variation between epochs or different architectures measures something else.

Limitation

Uncertainty in the ranking

Rank 1 classified 240 of the 288 test clips correctly, compared with 232 for rank 3. That is an eight-clip advantage, or 2.78 percentage points. To assess this difference, we would compare which clips each model got wrong and repeat training with different starting weights and data orders. This would help determine whether rank 1's advantage persists across runs.

The five models scored between 78.8% and 83.3% in these runs. Those are the lowest and highest scores observed. A newly trained model could score outside that range.

Shared architecture settings

All five candidates use two convolution blocks, 3×3 then 5×5 kernels, 48×48 inputs, 16 frames, no hidden fully connected layer, and average pooling after the first block. They differ in channel counts and in how the second block reduces spatial resolution.

Observation

Average pooling and stride-2 convolution

Rank 1 uses average pooling after the second convolution and reports 83.33% test accuracy at 50.1 million synaptic operations per clip. The four candidates using stride-2 convolution report 78.82% to 80.56% at 18.5 to 22.6 million operations.

Stride-2 convolution evaluates fewer spatial positions. Average pooling combines outputs after the convolution has evaluated those positions, which increases computation. Because channel counts also differ between these candidates, a matched experiment is needed to isolate the effect of pooling.

Interpretation

Absolute and relative operation counts

Synaptic operations (SynOps) estimate spike-triggered additions to downstream neurons per input clip. They are a measure of computational activity. Energy and latency were not measured for these candidates.

The dense ratio divides each candidate’s SynOps by the multiply-accumulate count for a conventional artificial neural network (ANN) of the same shape. Rank 1 has a ratio of 0.73, so its counted operations are 27% fewer than its own dense reference.

Each ratio uses a different reference: 69.0 million dense operations for rank 1 and 12.5 million for rank 4. A lower ratio does not imply fewer absolute operations across architectures. Additions and multiply-accumulates also have different energy costs.

Open

Choosing an accuracy–computation tradeoff

Rank 3 uses 20.3 million SynOps at 80.56% accuracy, compared with rank 1’s 50.1 million at 83.33%. The project has not set a budget that determines which is preferable. Its untested pareto search objective would retain configurations for which improving one objective requires worsening another.

7.4Effects of weight decay

A weight controls how strongly one neuron's output affects the next neuron. Weight decay adds a tendency for weights to move toward zero during training, alongside the updates that improve classification. A larger decay setting makes this tendency stronger.

This can reduce overfitting, where a model learns details specific to its training examples and performs less well on new examples. In a spiking network, smaller weights can also make neurons less likely to reach their firing threshold, reducing spike activity. Too much decay can weaken useful connections and lower accuracy.

To test these effects, the rank-1 architecture was trained for 300 epochs at each of three decay settings, with the other settings held fixed. Each setting was tested once, so the results do not show how much the scores would vary if training were repeated.

Observation

Fewer operations at weight decay 0.001

SynOps fell from 80.1 million to 50.1 million per clip, while test accuracy increased from 82.29% to 83.33%, a difference of three correctly classified clips. The operation-count change is substantial. The accuracy difference needs repeated runs before it can be attributed reliably to weight decay.

Spike counts also fell from 263,034 to 141,940 per clip. Smaller weights can reduce input currents and threshold crossings, which is consistent with this observation. The sweep did not directly isolate that mechanism.

Higher decay

Accuracy declined at weight decay 0.01

Training accuracy fell to 80.0% and test accuracy to 68.40%, compared with the published reference of 68.75%. Of the three settings tested, 0.001 had the highest test accuracy. Three values do not locate the optimum.

Weight-decay result

From 0.000094 to 0.001, counted operations fell 37.5% and test accuracy rose by three clips. At 0.01, accuracy declined. Repeated runs are needed to assess the small accuracy increase.

Across the 111 scored search trials, weight decay had a positive rank correlation with validation accuracy (ρ = 0.227, adjusted p-value = 0.0364). This association and the sweep concern the same project and are not independent replications. The benefit also did not extend to the largest decay value tested.

7.5Separating hyperparameter effects from limited sampling and implementation errors

A hyperparameter is a setting chosen before training, such as dropout strength or the weight-decay value. A low score alone does not show that a setting is ineffective. Here, the learning curves, trial counts, and implementation checks help distinguish a limited observed benefit from too few trials or an error in how the setting was applied.

Within this training budget

No delayed improvement within 300 epochs

Training accuracy plateaued near epoch 80. At the best tested decay, the final 216 epochs improved validation accuracy by about 0.6 percentage points.

Grokking describes a delayed improvement on unseen data after a model has already fitted its training data. These runs did not show that pattern within their training budget. A longer run would test whether improvement occurs later, but changing label smoothing or quantization at the same time would confound that comparison.

Early performance

Lower early accuracy with global average pooling

A flatten head retains each spatial position as a separate input to the classifier. Global average pooling (GAP) averages each channel over space, reducing classifier fan-in to the channel count. Reported rejection rates were 6% for GAP and 22% for flatten heads.

At the first stopping checkpoint, GAP reports 29.6% accuracy and a gain of 0.43 percentage points per epoch. Flatten heads report 65.1% and 0.63 points per epoch. Their eventual performance cannot be inferred from these stopped runs.

Insufficient evidence

Convolutional dropout needs more trials

Spatiotemporal dropout suppresses selected convolutional activations during training to reduce reliance on them. It appeared in 44 of 318 trials that passed the hardware checks, and only 12 reached a final score. The reported p-value of 0.50 does not establish either a benefit or no effect.

At the first checkpoint, 43% of trials with dropout stopped, compared with 47% without it. This does not suggest a large difference in early stopping. A balanced comparison with dropout enabled and disabled is more informative than comparing a new forced-on search with this adaptive search.

Implementation error

Output spikes were included in the activity penalty

The penalty discouraged spikes in every spiking layer, including the classifier. Since output spike rates are also the class scores used by the classification loss, this added an objective that could suppress the prediction signal.

Trials with the penalty stopped at the first checkpoint at a rate of 92%, compared with 43% without it. Their reported early accuracies were 33.2% and 62.6%. The output layer has since been excluded, but the effect of that fix still requires an experiment.

Confounding and adaptive sampling

Optuna adapts its sampling to earlier results, so promising settings appear together more often. In this run, the learning-rate schedule correlates with dropout at −0.57, leak constant at +0.46, and weight decay at +0.42.

When settings change together, separate tests cannot identify which setting caused a score difference. Adaptive sampling and early stopping also limit the interpretation of permutation p-values. Matched experiments are needed to test the individual effects.

The sampled settings were unbalanced: 93% of trials used depth 2, 95% had no hidden head layer, and 93% used 16 frames. These frequencies describe the search’s allocation of trials. They provide limited evidence for comparison with rarely sampled alternatives.

The narrative previously said all 30 GAP trials stopped at the first checkpoint, while the table reports 97%. An exact numerator and denominator are needed to resolve that discrepancy.

What early stopping can establish

To compare early performance, the analysis groups trials stopped at the same checkpoint. This avoids comparing scores after different training budgets. Early slopes can describe progress up to that checkpoint, but cannot determine how a stopped trial would perform after full training.

Repeated runs are still needed

The five candidate scores come from different architectures. The study reports no repeated final evaluations of one configuration across random seeds. Epoch-to-epoch score variation cannot substitute for that measurement or provide a calibrated confidence interval for a new run.

The current evidence is an observed range of 78.8% to 83.3% across five candidates. Repeated runs and a fully documented evaluation protocol are needed before making a broader performance claim.
