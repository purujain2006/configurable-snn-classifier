H.1Where these numbers come from

Three sets of runs produced the results on this page, all from the search on DVS128 Gesture described on page C.

the search
Every trial fits the chip from the start, trains for up to 80 epochs, and is scored on four people held out from training. At the snapshot of October 18, 2026, 163 of its 400 trials had finished, and the best scored 74.5% on validation.
the best five, trained long
The search's five best trials by validation score were each trained again from scratch for 300 epochs with the same settings, then measured once on the test set: 288 clips from six people the search never saw. Four had finished when these results were copied; the fifth was at epoch 160 of 300.
the best two, with augmentation
The two best were also trained for 300 epochs with augmentation switched on, to see whether it helps once training is long enough.

Every network on this page fits the chip, and for every one the chip simulator gave the same answer as the trained network on every validation clip.

The honest headline is 85.4%. The method picks the final network by its validation score and only then looks at the test set, and network 4 is that pick. Network 2's 86.8% is the best test score measured, but it was found by looking at the test scores of several networks, which makes it slightly optimistic. The two networks are one validation clip apart, so either could be the better one.

Both are far above the 68.75% in the HiAER-Spike paper, whose models use different input sizes and were measured on the FPGA, so the gap compares two different setups rather than two equivalent ones. They also beat every network trained before the chip check counted connections correctly (H.8), which means obeying the chip's limits has not cost accuracy.

What the table shows:

  • Longer training helps every network. Validation rose by 2 to 8 points between 80 and 300 epochs, by 4 to 17 clips.
  • The 80-epoch order did not survive. Network 1, the best in the search, is the weakest after 300 epochs, and network 4, tied for third, is the best. An 80-epoch score is an early, noisy reading, so a short run can pick the wrong winner. Retraining the finalists for longer than 80 epochs would make the final pick more reliable.
  • Test runs 4 to 7 points above validation, for all four. The four validation people are harder to recognize than the six test people. Validation is still the right score to choose with, because it never touches the test set, but it understates how well these networks do on new people. Five-fold cross-validation (B.6) gives every training person a turn and takes this bias out of the comparison.
  • The chip costs nothing measurable. Training already used the chip's arithmetic, export is exact, and the simulator matched the trained network on every clip.

H.4What the best networks have in common

Forgot some of these?frame · bit · kernel · pooling · stride · flatten · signed spikes · batch normalization · tdBN · leak · rate loss · learning-rate schedule · firing rate
frame
One slice of a recording, holding the events from a short stretch of time. A clip of T frames runs for T steps. B.2
bit
A binary digit, 0 or 1. With B bits a number can go from 0 to 2^B − 1: 1 bit gives 0 or 1, 4 bits give 0 to 15. E.1
kernel
The small grid of weights a convolution slides across the picture, for example 5 × 5. A.4
pooling
Shrinking a map by replacing each 2 × 2 block with one number: its average (average pool) or its largest value (max pool). A.4
stride
How far a kernel moves at each step. A stride of 2 roughly halves the size of the map. A.4
flatten
Laying every cell of every map out in one line, so the classifier knows where each feature appeared. A.4
signed spikes
Spikes that can be +1 or −1. On the chip each signed unit is two neurons, one for each sign. A.11
batch normalization (BN)
A training step that holds each channel's values in a steady range. It subtracts the channel's average over the batch, divides by its spread, then applies a learned scale and shift. After training it is a fixed multiply and add, which is folded into the weights and thresholds before deployment. A.12
tdBN
Threshold-dependent batch normalization, a version of batch norm made for spiking networks. It works out one average and spread over all the timesteps of a clip together, and sizes its output relative to the neuron's threshold. A.12
leak (τ)
How fast a spiking neuron's potential fades, set by τ (tau): each step it loses 1/τ of itself. On the chip τ must be a power of two. A.5
rate loss
Scoring a spiking network by its output spike counts over the whole clip, turned into probabilities with softmax and scored with cross-entropy. It is what the chip reports. A.15
learning-rate schedule
A rule for changing the learning rate over a run, usually from larger to smaller, for example smoothly (cosine) or in stages (step). A.14
firing rate
How often neurons fire. A firing-rate penalty adds it to the loss, so the network learns to fire less and do less work. A.15

The sampler has settled on one design. Networks 2 to 5, and in fact eleven of the twelve best networks the search has trained so far, share every major choice:

  • Input: 40 × 40 frames, one bit per pixel, and T = 48 frames per clip, the longest clips the search allows.
  • Shape: four layers with kernels of 9, 7, 5 and 3. The first two layers are narrow, 8 to 19 channels, and the last two wide, mostly 40 to 120. Layer 1 shrinks the map with an average pool, layer 3 with a stride, and the head flattens with no hidden layer.
  • Neurons: signed spikes, the bias folded into the threshold, tdBN normalization, and τ learned once per layer.
  • Training: the rate loss, a step learning-rate schedule, the firing-rate penalty on, and augmentation off.

Two of these say where to look next. Every one of the twelve best uses T = 48, the largest value the search was allowed to try, so the best number of frames may be larger still. And the learned leak grew: started at τ = 8, the convolution layers ended at τ = 64 or 128, losing less than 2% of their potential per step. The best networks chose neurons that barely forget, so evidence from the whole clip adds up before they fire.

In the search recordsdeployed_tau

deployed_tau lists the leak τ each spiking layer runs with on the chip; a larger τ forgets more slowly. Network 2 deployed with deployed_tau = 128,128,128,64,4: τ of 128 in its first three layers, 64 in the fourth, and 4 in the output layer.

H.5Overfitting, and what augmentation did

Without augmentation, every network reaches 98 to 100% accuracy on its training clips by epoch 150, while validation stays below 81%. The networks memorize the 19 training people. No run showed a late jump in validation after training accuracy reached its ceiling (sometimes called grokking), within 300 epochs.

Networks 1 and 2 were trained again with augmentation on (shifts up to a tenth of the frame, a blanked square up to a fifth of its side, and one event in ten dropped).

Augmentation did what it is meant to do on the training side: the networks stopped memorizing, ending at 86 to 93% on their own clips instead of 100%. It has not yet turned that into better validation scores, which moved by one clip. On test it helped network 1 by 14 clips and cost network 2 four. With one run each, that is not a verdict. It matches the balanced comparison on page D, where augmentation hurt at 80 epochs, and suggests augmentation needs even longer training, or several seeds, before its effect can be judged.

H.6How much work they do

Forgot some of these?SynOp · multiply-add · flush steps · firing rate · signed spikes
SynOp (synaptic operation)
One spike adding one weight to one neuron: the unit of work on a spiking chip, used as a stand-in for energy. A.9
multiply-add (MAC)
One multiplication and one addition: the unit of work in an ordinary network. A.9
flush steps
Extra empty steps after the last frame, one per layer, so the evidence from the last frames can still reach the output. A.7
firing rate
How often neurons fire. A firing-rate penalty adds it to the loss, so the network learns to fire less and do less work. A.15
signed spikes
Spikes that can be +1 or −1. On the chip each signed unit is two neurons, one for each sign. A.11

The point of a spiking chip is to do less work than a conventional network. These networks do not, yet. They run 53 steps per clip (48 frames and 5 flush steps), at least half their neurons fire on a typical step, and their measured SynOps per clip come to 4.4 to 6.2 times the multiply-adds of one pass of a conventional network of the same shape.

Those ratios are a floor. The SynOps counter that ran with these networks did not yet count signed spikes correctly: a −1 spike could cancel a +1 spike in the count, so the true count is higher. The saved networks can be recounted with the corrected counter. Whatever the exact figure, accuracy has so far come from long clips and busy neurons, and the next step is a search that weighs work as well as accuracy, through the firing-rate penalty or a SynOps budget (page G).

In the search recordssynops_per_samplesynops_over_dense

Network 4 recorded synops_per_sample = 97,100,000, about 97 million spike-triggered additions per clip, and synops_over_dense = 6.2: 6.2 times the multiply-adds of one pass of a conventional network of its shape. Both are undercounts for signed spikes.

H.7How far to trust these numbers

  • Each network was trained once, with one seed. Two runs of the same network can differ by several points (page B), and networks 2, 3 and 4 sit within 2.5 points of each other on test.
  • The test set has now been looked at for six networks. The validation-chosen 85.4% is the fair headline; the highest test score among several is optimistic.
  • The search was still running when these results were copied, so its best networks may change.
  • Scores come from the chip simulator. Energy, timing and accuracy on the physical FPGA have not been measured.
  • SynOps for signed networks are undercounts until they are recounted (H.6).

H.8Earlier networks

Before the chip check counted connections in front of pools, an earlier 400-configuration search found networks that scored up to 83.3% on test after 300 epochs, but the most accurate of them needs 32,256 incoming connections per output neuron against the chip's 8,159, so it cannot run on the chip. The best of them that fits scored 79.2%. That search also chose with validation clips drawn from the same people as training, which made its validation scores run about 9 points high. The networks on this page beat both numbers while fitting the chip.

Two measurements from those networks still help. The most accurate one did 50.1 million SynOps per clip at T = 16, 0.73 times the work of a conventional network of its shape, which shows what shorter clips buy. And raising its weight decay from 0.0001 to 0.001 cut its SynOps by 37% while gaining three test clips, although weight decay also changed its leak τ in those runs.
