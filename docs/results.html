H.1Where these numbers come from

Two sets of experiments produced the results on this site, and they answer different questions.

the search under way
The DVS128 Gesture search on page C: every trial fits the chip by construction, trains for up to 80 epochs, and is scored on four people held out from training. Its best network so far, the example network used throughout this site, scores 74.5% on validation. Its first 96 trials are also the balanced comparison behind every measurement on page D. It has not finished, so it has no test score yet.
five networks trained long
Five networks from an earlier 400-configuration search, each trained again for 300 epochs and then measured once on the test people. That search did not yet count fan-in in front of pools and drew its validation clips from the same people as training, which is why the most accurate of the five does not fit the chip and why only test scores are quoted for them.

So the test numbers below are the best measured so far, and the search under way is how a network that fits the chip and scores higher is meant to be found.

The most accurate network has two convolution layers: 81 channels with a 3×3 kernel, then 56 channels with a 5×5 kernel, each followed by a 2×2 average pool, on 48×48 frames with 16 frames per clip. On the simulator it classifies 240 of the 288 test clips correctly. Its PyTorch model and the simulator predict the same gesture on every one of the 464 validation and test clips.

It learned τ = 3 in every layer, which the chip cannot run (page F). At τ = 4, the nearest power of two, its test accuracy stays at 83.3%. It still does not fit the chip: its two average pools fold into the output layer, so each of its 11 output neurons needs 32,256 connections against a limit of 8,159 (page G).

The paper's models use different input sizes and were measured on the FPGA, so the 14.6-point gap compares two different setups, not two equivalent ones. The most accurate network that fits the chip, network 3 below, scores 79.2%.

The five networks below share one design: two convolution layers (3×3, then 5×5), 48×48 input, 16 frames, no hidden head layer, and an average pool after the first layer. Each was trained for 300 epochs with weight decay 0.001. They differ in channel counts and in how the second layer shrinks the map. Rows are in order of test accuracy.

In the tool's records the Test column is hw_test_accuracy and the SynOps column is synops_per_sample. Network 1's 0.8333 is 240 of the 288 test clips, the amber squares below, with the six test people in alternating outlines; its 50.1M is about 50 million spike-triggered additions per clip.

The pool after the first layer is what decides which networks fit. It multiplies the second layer's fan-in by four, to 5 × 5 × C × 4 for C first-layer channels, which stays under 8,159 only up to 81 channels. Networks 2 and 4 have 84 and 91. Network 1 passes that check with exactly 81 and then fails at the head, behind its second pool.

Networks 3 and 5 fit. On the chip they run at the nearest power of two to the τ they learned: network 3 at τ = 32 scores 79.2% (228 clips), and network 5 at τ = 4 scores 78.8%, with its PyTorch model and the simulator agreeing on every clip.

Network 1 is the most accurate and also the most active: 50.1 million SynOps per clip, against 18 to 23 million for the others. Its 0.73× ratio only says it does fewer operations than a conventional network of its own shape; each row's ratio has a different denominator, so the ratios cannot rank the networks against each other. If operation count matters, network 2 classifies 232 clips correctly with 20.3 million SynOps, eight fewer clips than network 1 for 40% of the work.

H.4What weight decay did

Weight decay pulls parameters toward zero during training, which can reduce overfitting and, in a spiking network, lower firing. Network 1 was trained three times for 300 epochs, changing only the weight decay. In these runs weight decay also applied to τ, so the last column shows the τ each layer learned, starting from 8.

Raising weight decay from 0.000096 to 0.001 cut SynOps by 37% and gained three test clips. At 0.01 the network stopped fitting its training data (80% training accuracy), and test accuracy fell to 68.4%, just below the paper's 68.75%.

The τ column complicates the reading. As weight decay rose it drove τ down, and a smaller τ makes neurons forget faster and fire less, so part of the drop in SynOps may come from τ and not from the weights. Weight decay applies only to connection weights in the tool's training (page F), so repeating this sweep would separate the two effects.

No run showed a late jump in test accuracy after training accuracy had flattened (delayed generalization, sometimes called grokking) within 300 epochs. Training accuracy leveled off near epoch 80.

H.5How far to trust these numbers

  • Each network was trained once. Without repeated runs there is no measure of how much a score moves by chance, and the five networks above sit within a few points of each other (page B shows how much one score can move).
  • These networks were chosen and checkpointed with a validation set that shared people with training. That validation ran about 9 points above test, so only test accuracy is quoted here.
  • Test results have guided which experiments came next, so test accuracy has partly been used for selection.
  • Scores come from the simulator. Energy, timing and accuracy on the physical FPGA have not been measured.

Which settings matter for accuracy is measured separately, on a balanced comparison of 96 networks, each trained for 80 epochs and scored on the simulator on held-out people. Page D shows the results setting by setting.
