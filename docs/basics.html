Neural networks, from the ground up

What an artificial neuron computes, how a network of them learns, why image networks slide small filters across the picture, and what changes when neurons talk in spikes instead of numbers. Every idea comes with a picture you can play with.

A.1One artificial neuron

An artificial neuron is a small calculation. It takes a few numbers in, multiplies each by its own weight, adds the results together with one more number called the bias, and passes the total through an activation function. The most common activation, ReLU, keeps a positive total and turns a negative one into zero.

The weights are what a network learns. A large positive weight means "this input pushes me to respond", a negative one means "this input argues against it", and a weight near zero means "ignore this input".

Make weight 2 strongly negative and input 2 large: the total drops below zero and ReLU silences the neuron. That on and off behavior, repeated across thousands of neurons, is what lets a network make complicated decisions.

A.2Layers, and one pass through a network

Neurons are arranged in layers. Each neuron in a layer reads every output of the layer before it, so information flows from the input, through the hidden layers, to the output layer, which has one neuron per class. Running one input through the network is called a forward pass.

The output layer gives one score per class. A function called softmax turns the scores into probabilities that add up to 100%, and the network's answer is the class with the highest probability.

ANIMATION A forward pass through four layers Brighter neurons are more active. The pulses show how much of each neuron's output each connection carries forward.

The weights never change between inputs here, only the input does. A trained network is exactly this: a fixed function from an input to an answer. Training is what picked the weights.

A.3How a network learns

Learning means adjusting the weights until the answers come out right. Three ideas do the work.

Loss
One number that says how wrong the network was on an example. The usual choice for classification, cross-entropy, is small when the right class got a high probability and large when it did not.
Gradient
For every weight, which direction to nudge it to make the loss smaller, and how strongly. An algorithm called backpropagation computes all of them at once by working backwards from the output.
Gradient descent
Move every weight a small step downhill, then repeat. The size of the step is the learning rate.

Training never looks at the whole dataset at once. It takes a small random batch of examples (16 clips in this project), computes the loss and gradient on that batch alone, takes one step, and moves to the next batch. One pass over all of the training data is an epoch, and a training run is many epochs.

A.4Convolutional networks

Pictures have structure that a plain layer ignores. Neighboring pixels belong together, and a pattern means the same thing wherever it appears. A plain layer on a 40 × 40 picture would also need 1,600 weights for every neuron. A convolution handles all three problems.

A convolution slides a small grid of weights, the kernel, across the picture. At each position it multiplies the pixels under the kernel by the kernel's weights and adds them up, exactly like the single neuron above. The results form a new picture, a feature map, which is bright wherever the picture looks like the kernel. The same few weights are reused at every position.

ANIMATION A 3 × 3 kernel sliding over a picture Each step multiplies the 9 pixels under the window by the 9 kernel weights and writes the sum into the feature map. Amber is positive and rust is negative.

Click pixels in the picture to draw your own shape. The vertical-edge kernel turns amber where a bar begins and rust where it ends, and the horizontal one does the same from top to bottom. A trained network learns its kernels instead of being given them.

In the search records

Each layer's kernel size is a setting, named k_ plus the layer's number, counting from 0. The examples on this site use one real network, the best of the first 96 networks the search trained (page C). In it, k_0 = 5, k_1 = 5 and k_2 = 3: the first two layers slide a 5 × 5 window and the third a 3 × 3 one. So k_1 is the second layer's window, highlighted below. Point at a name to move the highlight.

Channels

One kernel finds one kind of pattern, so a layer has many kernels, one per output channel, and each produces its own feature map. The kernels of the next layer read all of those maps at once, so they can combine "edge here" and "corner there" into larger shapes. Early layers find edges and blobs; later ones respond to parts of hands and arms.

In the search records

ch_0 = 71 means the first layer has 71 kernels and so makes 71 feature maps, drawn as the depth of its block. There are three convolution layers, so depth = 3.

Shrinking the map

Each layer usually makes the map smaller, so later layers see more of the picture per cell and the network stays affordable. There are two ways to do it. A stride of 2 moves the kernel two cells at a time instead of one. Pooling runs the convolution normally and then replaces each 2 × 2 block of the result with one number: its average, or its largest value.

ANIMATION Three ways to shrink a map of spikes Amber cells on the left are neurons that fired. The highlighted window shows which cells feed the highlighted output.

In the search records

ds_ plus a layer number says how that layer shrinks its map: stride, pool or none. In the same network ds_0 = stride turns the 40 × 40 input into an 18 × 18 map, ds_1 = none keeps it close to that size (14 × 14, since a kernel cannot hang over the edge), and ds_2 = stride halves it again to 6 × 6. pool_type (avg or max) only matters when some layer pools.

The head

After the last convolution, the feature maps have to become 11 scores, one per gesture. Flatten lays every cell of every map out in a line and connects all of them to the classifier, so the classifier knows where each feature appeared. Global average pooling, or GAP, averages each map down to one number, so the classifier knows how much of each feature there was but not where.

On the chip, GAP does not save connections. An average has no neurons of its own, so it is folded into the next layer's connections, and every class neuron still connects to every neuron of the last convolution layer (page G). GAP still has far fewer weights to learn, which can reduce overfitting. Between the head and the 11 outputs there can also be one hidden layer of spiking neurons.

In the search records

final_reduction = flatten in the example network: its 41 maps of 6 × 6 reach the classifier as 1,476 numbers. One hidden layer of 128 neurons follows, which the record writes as fc_layers = 1 and fc_width_0 = 128, and num_classes = 11 is one output neuron per gesture.

A.5Spiking neurons

The neurons above pass around numbers like 0.73. Neurons in a brain, and on a neuromorphic chip such as HiAER-Spike, pass around spikes: at each moment a neuron either fires or it does not. So a spiking network runs through time. The input arrives as a sequence of T frames, every layer updates once per frame, and the answer comes from counting spikes.

The spiking neuron used here is the leaky integrate-and-fire neuron. It keeps one number, its membrane potential, and at every timestep it does four things, in this order:

  1. FireIf the potential is above the threshold, send a spike.
  2. ResetA neuron that fired drops back to zero.
  3. LeakThe potential loses a fraction 1/τ of itself, so old input fades away. τ (tau) is the leak constant.
  4. IntegrateAdd the weights of the input spikes that just arrived.

The picture below puts the neuron from A.1 next to its spiking version, with the same inputs and the same weights. The only change in the inputs is how they arrive: a value of 0.8 becomes a spike on 80% of the steps. Its weighted sum becomes the membrane potential, which keeps adding up what arrives step after step, and ReLU becomes a threshold: fire, or stay silent.

The artificial neuron answers at once with one exact number. The spiking neuron answers with a stream of spikes, and the number only shows up as how often it fires. Raise τ and it remembers longer, so scattered inputs can add up to a spike; raise the threshold and it needs more input. Because it checks for a spike before adding new input, an input arriving now can only cause an output spike on the next step.

The exact arithmetic on the chip, worked by hand

The chip stores the potential as a whole number, so two details matter. It fires only when the potential is strictly greater than θ, and the leak is rounded down: each step the potential loses v/τ, rounded down to a whole step of the chip's grid. A potential smaller than τ grid steps therefore does not leak at all.

Start at v = 0 with τ = 4, θ = 1, and an input of 0.6 on every step.

  1. Step 1: 0 is below θ, so no spike. The leak leaves 0, and the input raises v to 0.6.
  2. Step 2: 0.6 is below θ. The leak removes 0.6 / 4 = 0.15, and the input raises v to 1.05.
  3. Step 3: 1.05 is above θ, so the neuron fires and resets to 0. The input raises v to 0.6 again.

The spike comes one step after the potential first passed 1. Each layer of a network adds one such step. Page F shows how training reproduces this exact arithmetic, rounding included, so that the trained network and the chip agree spike for spike.

In the search records

tau = 4: every neuron loses a quarter of its potential each step. Whether τ stays at that value (fixed) or is learned once per layer (layer) or separately for every channel (channel) is set by tau_mode, and whether θ is learned as well by trainable_threshold. All three set the spiking neurons, marked by the dots above each layer.

A.6Spiking convolutions

A convolution works the same way with spikes. The kernel slides over the input exactly as in A.4, but each cell of the output is now a spiking neuron: the weighted sum under the kernel is added to that cell's membrane potential, the potential leaks, and the cell fires when it crosses the threshold. A new input frame arrives every step, so the layer produces a new map of spikes every step, and counting each cell's spikes over the clip gives a picture much like the ordinary feature map.

ANIMATION An edge-detecting kernel on a moving bar, one step at a time Left to right: this step's input spikes, the kernel, every output cell's membrane potential (amber is positive, rust negative), the cells that fired this step, and each cell's spike count so far.

The kernel is the vertical-edge detector from A.4, flipped to respond where bright turns to dark, and it is applied at every position at once on every step. Cells at the bar's front edge build up potential and fire on the next step; cells behind the bar are pushed negative and stay silent. As the bar sweeps across, its front edge leaves a trail of spikes, and the count map on the right is the spiking layer's version of a feature map, made of whole numbers.

A.7A spiking network, step by step

Put layers of these neurons together and run them over a clip. Each frame's spikes go into the first layer, that layer's spikes feed the next, and the output layer has one neuron per class. The output spikes are counted over the whole clip, and the class whose neuron fired most is the answer.

Each layer adds one step of delay, because a neuron only reacts on the step after its input. So after the last frame the network runs a few more empty steps, one per layer, to let the evidence from the last frames reach the output. These are the flush steps.

The output counts nothing for the first two steps: activity needs one step to reach the hidden layer and another to reach the output. More frames give the network more evidence before it has to answer, at the cost of more time and work.

Without flush steps, the last frames of every clip never reach the output, and a deeper network loses more of them. Each max pool also adds a flush step, because its OR neurons (page G) are a layer of chip neurons too. The output count is divided by T, so scores stay comparable between networks of different depths.

A.8What turning numbers into spikes costs

An artificial neuron passes on a precise number, say 0.37. A spiking neuron can only fire or stay silent on each step, so it has to spread that number over time, firing on about 37% of the steps. With T steps it can only say one of T + 1 things (0 of T, 1 of T, and so on), so every answer is rounded to the nearest step of 1/T. Three more losses come from how the neuron itself works:

Too weak to fire
The leak drains a weak input faster than it builds up, so the potential never reaches the threshold and a small but real value comes out as exactly zero.
Reset to zero
When the neuron fires, whatever the potential overshot the threshold by is thrown away instead of being carried into the next step.
At most one spike per step
Every value above the threshold comes out the same: a spike on every step.

Press sweep and watch the staircase: flat at zero for weak inputs, then jumping in steps of 1/T, then flat again at one spike per step. More frames make the steps finer but cost more time and work on the chip. Without a leak and with the overshoot carried over, the staircase hugs the line; the chip resets to zero and leaks, so its staircase sits further from the line.

Every layer passes its rounded answer on to the next, so the errors compound through the network. This is the main reason a spiking network usually scores somewhat below an ordinary network of the same shape, and why converting an already trained ordinary network into a spiking one loses accuracy. Training the network as a spiking network from the start, as this project does, lets the weights adapt to these limits, which recovers much of the gap but not all of it. The all-or-nothing spike is also why training needs a trick to compute gradients (A.10).

Is this what limits accuracy here?

Mostly not. The networks here reach 90 to 99% accuracy on their own training data, so turning numbers into spikes does not stop them from learning the gestures. What holds validation accuracy below about 80% is overfitting: the networks learn the 19 training people rather than the gestures (page B). Binarizing still costs something, and more frames would shrink that cost at the price of time and energy on the chip.

A.9Why spikes can save work

In an ordinary network every connection multiplies its input on every pass, whether or not the input carries anything. In a spiking network nothing happens unless a neuron fires, and a spike only adds its weight to the neurons it connects to. A layer in which few neurons fire does very little work. That is the case for neuromorphic hardware: the work, and so the energy, grows with activity rather than with the size of the network.

Each lit connection on the spiking side is one synaptic operation (SynOp): one spike adding one weight to one neuron. Counting SynOps is the usual way to compare how much work spiking networks do.

A.10Training through a spike

A spike is a step: below the threshold the output is 0, above it the output is 1. The slope of a step is zero everywhere except at the jump, where it is infinite, so gradient descent gets no useful direction from it. Spiking networks are trained with a surrogate gradient instead. The forward pass keeps the real step, so the network behaves exactly as it will on the chip. Only when the gradient is computed is the step treated as a smooth curve, whose slope tells each weight "this neuron was close to firing, and here is which way to push it".

The stand-in used here is the slope of an arctangent curve. A neuron that came close to firing gets a large gradient, which matches the intuition that a small weight change there would have flipped the spike, and a neuron far from θ gets almost none. The same trick, passing a gradient through a rounding step as if the rounding were not there, is called the straight-through estimator. Training uses it for the chip's integer weights (page G) and for its rounded-down leak.

A.11Signed spikes

An ordinary spiking neuron can only say "yes". With signed spikes, a unit can also fire a negative spike. On the chip such a unit is two neurons: one integrates the input, the other integrates its negative, and whichever crosses its threshold sends a +1 or a −1. A layer can then say "strongly not this" as well as "strongly this", at the cost of twice the neurons and up to four times the connections.

The second neuron's outgoing connections carry negated weights, so the next layer receives +w or −w. Every neuron of a signed unit connects to both neurons of each unit in the next layer, which is why the neurons double and the connections can quadruple, and the chip check (page G) counts them that way. A max pool stays exact with one OR neuron per sign.

In the search records

signed_spikes = True in the example network: every spiking layer, marked ±, is built from pairs of chip neurons.

In short

An artificial neuron is a weighted sum and an activation, and a network is layers of them, trained by gradient descent on a loss. Convolutions reuse small kernels across the picture, stride and pooling shrink the maps, and the head turns maps into class scores. Spiking neurons and spiking convolutions do the same job with spikes over time: fire, reset, leak, integrate, and count the output spikes. Squeezing numbers into spikes rounds them and costs some accuracy. Training uses a smooth stand-in for the spike's slope.
