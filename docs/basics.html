Neural networks, from the ground up

What an artificial neuron computes, how a network of them learns, why image networks slide small filters across the picture, and what changes when neurons talk in spikes instead of numbers. Every idea comes with a picture you can play with.

A.1One artificial neuron

Why this exists

Some tasks are easy for people and nearly impossible to write down as rules. Exactly which pixels make a hand wave? Hands, distances and speeds all differ from one person to the next. A neural network gets around this. It is built from many small adjustable calculations and learns the rule by tuning them on recorded examples. Each calculation is kept simple so that huge numbers of them can be combined and tuned automatically.

An artificial neuron is a small calculation. It takes a few numbers in, multiplies each by its own weight, adds the results together with one more number called the bias, and passes the total through an activation function. The most common activation, ReLU, keeps a positive total and turns a negative one into zero.

The weights are what a network learns. A large positive weight means "this input pushes me to respond", a negative one means "this input argues against it", and a weight near zero means "ignore this input".

A.2Layers, and one pass through a network

Why this exists

One neuron can only split its inputs along a single straight line, more of this and less of that. Hard tasks need more. Stacked layers let later neurons combine the simple patterns earlier ones found, edges into strokes and strokes into a hand, which is where a network's power comes from.

Neurons are arranged in layers. Each neuron in a layer reads every output of the layer before it, so information flows from the input, through the hidden layers, to the output layer, which has one neuron per class. Running one input through the network is called a forward pass.

The output layer gives one score per class. A function called softmax turns the scores into probabilities that add up to 100%, and the network's answer is the class with the highest probability.

ANIMATION A forward pass through four layers Brighter neurons are more active. The pulses show how much of each neuron's output each connection carries forward.

The weights never change between inputs here, only the input does. A trained network is exactly this, a fixed function from an input to an answer. Training is what picked the weights.

A.3How a network learns

Why this exists

Even a small network has thousands of weights, and the networks here have far more, so nobody could set them by hand. Training sets them automatically, from examples whose right answers are known.

Learning means adjusting the weights until the answers come out right. Three ideas do the work.

Loss
One number that says how wrong the network was on an example. The usual choice for classification, cross-entropy, is small when the right class got a high probability and large when it did not.
Gradient
For every weight, which direction to nudge it to make the loss smaller, and how strongly. An algorithm called backpropagation computes all of them at once by working backwards from the output.
Gradient descent
Move every weight a small step downhill, then repeat. The size of the step is the learning rate.

Training never looks at the whole dataset at once. It takes a small random batch of examples (16 clips in this project), computes the loss and gradient on that batch alone, takes one step, and moves to the next batch. One pass over all of the training data is an epoch, and a training run is many epochs.

A.4Convolutional networks

Pictures have structure that a plain layer ignores. Neighboring pixels belong together, and a pattern means the same thing wherever it appears. A plain layer on a 40 × 40 picture would also need 1,600 weights for every neuron. A convolution handles all three problems.

A convolution slides a small grid of weights, the kernel, across the picture. At each position it multiplies the pixels under the kernel by the kernel's weights and adds them up, exactly like the single neuron above. The results form a new picture, a feature map, which is bright wherever the picture looks like the kernel. The same few weights are reused at every position.

Click pixels in the picture to draw your own shape. The vertical-edge kernel turns blue where a bar begins and orange where it ends, and the horizontal one does the same from top to bottom. A trained network learns its kernels instead of being given them.

In the search records

The project's search (page C) trains hundreds of networks and saves each one as a record, a list of short names, each with a value. Boxes like this one show how an idea appears in those records, always with the same real network as the example. Each layer's kernel size is named k_ plus the layer's number, counting from 0. In the example network, k_0 = 5, k_1 = 5 and k_2 = 3: the first two layers slide a 5 × 5 window and the third a 3 × 3 one. So k_1 is the second layer's window, highlighted below. Point at a name to move the highlight.

Channels

One kernel finds one kind of pattern, so a layer has many kernels, one per output channel, and each produces its own feature map. The kernels of the next layer read all of those maps at once, so they can combine "edge here" and "corner there" into larger shapes. Early layers find edges and blobs. Later ones respond to parts of hands and arms.

In the search records

ch_0 = 71 means the first layer has 71 kernels and so makes 71 feature maps, drawn as the depth of its block. There are three convolution layers, so depth = 3.

Shrinking the map

Each layer usually makes the map smaller, so later layers see more of the picture per cell and the network stays affordable. There are two ways to do it. A stride of 2 moves the kernel two cells at a time instead of one. Pooling runs the convolution normally and then replaces each 2 × 2 block of the result with one number, either its average or its largest value.

In the search records

ds_ plus a layer number says how that layer shrinks its map, with stride, pool or none. In the example network, ds_0 = stride shrinks the 40 × 40 input to 18 × 18. The second layer has ds_1 = none: it does not shrink on purpose, though the map still loses a thin border, because the 5 × 5 window cannot hang over the edge, so 18 × 18 becomes 14 × 14. Then ds_2 = stride shrinks it again, to 6 × 6.

The head

After the last convolution, the feature maps have to become 11 scores, one per gesture. Flatten lays every cell of every map out in a line and connects all of them to the classifier, so the classifier knows where each feature appeared. Global average pooling, or GAP, averages each map down to one number, so the classifier knows how much of each feature there was but not where.

On the chip, GAP does not save connections. An average has no neurons of its own, so it is folded into the next layer's connections, and every class neuron still connects to every neuron of the last convolution layer (page G). GAP still has far fewer weights to learn, which can reduce overfitting. Between the head and the 11 outputs there can also be one hidden layer of spiking neurons.

In the search records

final_reduction = flatten in the example network, so its 41 maps of 6 × 6 reach the classifier as 1,476 numbers. Then comes one hidden layer of 128 neurons, which the record writes as fc_layers = 1 (one hidden layer) and fc_width_0 = 128 (its width), and finally the 11 output neurons, one per gesture.

A.5Spiking neurons

Why this exists

Page 0 explains the reason. On an ordinary computer most of a network's energy goes into moving numbers around, and a chip whose neurons send brief spikes only when something happens avoids most of that traffic. The cost is that a value can no longer travel in one message. It has to be spread over time as a pattern of spikes, so a spiking network runs step by step.

The neurons above pass around numbers like 0.73. Neurons in a brain, and on a neuromorphic chip such as HiAER-Spike, pass around spikes: at each moment a neuron either fires or it does not. So a spiking network runs through time. The input arrives as a sequence of T frames, every layer updates once per frame, and the answer comes from counting spikes.

The spiking neuron used here is the leaky integrate-and-fire neuron. It keeps one number, its membrane potential, and at every timestep it does four things, in this order:

  1. FireIf the potential is above the threshold, send a spike.
  2. ResetA neuron that fired drops back to zero.
  3. LeakThe potential loses a fraction 1/τ of itself, so old input fades away. τ (tau) is the leak constant.
  4. IntegrateAdd the weights of the input spikes that just arrived.

The picture below puts the neuron from A.1 next to its spiking version, with the same inputs and the same weights. The only change in the inputs is how they arrive. A value of 0.8 becomes a spike on 80% of the steps. Its weighted sum becomes the membrane potential, which keeps adding up what arrives step after step, and ReLU becomes a threshold that decides whether to fire or stay silent.

The artificial neuron answers at once with one exact number. The spiking neuron answers with a stream of spikes, and the number only shows up as how often it fires. Raise τ and it remembers longer, so scattered inputs can add up to a spike. Raise the threshold and it needs more input. Because it checks for a spike before adding new input, an input arriving now can only cause an output spike on the next step.

The exact arithmetic on the chip, worked by hand

The chip stores the potential as a whole number, so two details matter. It fires only when the potential is strictly greater than θ, and the leak is rounded down. Each step the potential loses v/τ, rounded down to a whole step of the chip's grid. A potential smaller than τ grid steps therefore does not leak at all.

Start at v = 0 with τ = 4, θ = 1, and an input of 0.6 on every step.

  1. Step 1: 0 is below θ, so no spike. The leak leaves 0, and the input raises v to 0.6.
  2. Step 2: 0.6 is below θ. The leak removes 0.6 / 4 = 0.15, and the input raises v to 1.05.
  3. Step 3: 1.05 is above θ, so the neuron fires and resets to 0. The input raises v to 0.6 again.

The spike comes one step after the potential first passed 1. Each layer of a network adds one such step. Page F shows how training reproduces this exact arithmetic, rounding included, so that the trained network and the chip agree spike for spike.

In the search records

tau = 4 in the example network, so every spiking neuron loses a quarter of its potential each step. The spiking neurons are marked by the dots above each layer. Training can also be allowed to adjust τ. Page F covers that.

A.6Spiking convolutions

A convolution works the same way with spikes. The kernel slides over the input exactly as in A.4, but each cell of the output is now a spiking neuron. The weighted sum under the kernel is added to that cell's membrane potential, the potential leaks, and the cell fires when it crosses the threshold. A new input frame arrives every step, so the layer produces a new map of spikes every step, and counting each cell's spikes over the clip gives a picture much like the ordinary feature map.

ANIMATION An edge-detecting kernel on a moving bar, one step at a time From left to right, this step's input spikes, the kernel, every output cell's membrane potential (blue is positive, orange negative), the cells that fired this step, and each cell's spike count so far.

The kernel is the vertical-edge detector from A.4, flipped to respond where bright turns to dark, and it is applied at every position at once on every step. Cells at the bar's front edge build up potential and fire on the next step. Cells behind the bar are pushed negative and stay silent. As the bar sweeps across, its front edge leaves a trail of spikes, and the count map on the right is the spiking layer's version of a feature map, made of whole numbers.

A.7A spiking network, step by step

Put layers of these neurons together and run them over a clip. Each frame's spikes go into the first layer, that layer's spikes feed the next, and the output layer has one neuron per class. The output spikes are counted over the whole clip, and the class whose neuron fired most is the answer.

Each layer adds one step of delay, because a neuron only reacts on the step after its input. So after the last frame the network runs a few more empty steps, one per layer, to let the evidence from the last frames reach the output. These are the flush steps.

Without flush steps, the last frames of every clip never reach the output, and a deeper network loses more of them. Each max pool also adds a flush step, because its OR neurons (page G) are a layer of chip neurons too. The output count is divided by T, so scores stay comparable between networks of different depths.

A.8What turning numbers into spikes costs

An artificial neuron passes on a precise number, say 0.37. A spiking neuron can only fire or stay silent on each step, so it has to spread that number over time, firing on about 37% of the steps. With T steps it can only say one of T + 1 things (0 of T, 1 of T, and so on), so every answer is rounded to the nearest step of 1/T. Three more losses come from how the neuron itself works:

Too weak to fire
The leak drains a weak input faster than it builds up, so the potential never reaches the threshold and a small but real value comes out as exactly zero.
Reset to zero
When the neuron fires, whatever the potential overshot the threshold by is thrown away instead of being carried into the next step.
At most one spike per step
Every value above the threshold comes out the same, as a spike on every step.

Press sweep and watch the staircase. It is flat at zero for weak inputs, then jumping in steps of 1/T, then flat again at one spike per step. More frames make the steps finer but cost more time and work on the chip. Without a leak and with the overshoot carried over, the staircase hugs the line, but the chip resets to zero and leaks, so its staircase sits further from the line.

Every layer passes its rounded answer on to the next, so the errors compound through the network. This is the main reason a spiking network usually scores somewhat below an ordinary network of the same shape, and why converting an already trained ordinary network into a spiking one loses accuracy. Training the network as a spiking network from the start, as this project does, lets the weights adapt to these limits, which recovers much of the gap but not all of it. The all-or-nothing spike is also why training needs a trick to compute gradients (A.10).

Is this what limits accuracy here?

Mostly not. The networks here reach 90 to 99% accuracy on their own training data, so turning numbers into spikes does not stop them from learning the gestures. What holds validation accuracy below about 80% is overfitting. The networks learn the 19 training people instead of the gestures (page B). Binarizing still costs something, and more frames would shrink that cost at the price of time and energy on the chip.

A.9Why spikes can save work

In an ordinary network every connection multiplies its input on every pass, whether or not the input carries anything. This is called dense computation. In a spiking network nothing happens unless a neuron fires, and a spike only adds its weight to the neurons it connects to. A layer in which few neurons fire does very little work. That is the reason for neuromorphic hardware. The work, and so the energy, grows with activity and not with the size of the network.

A.10Training through a spike

A spike is a step. Below the threshold the output is 0, and above it the output is 1. The slope of a step is zero everywhere except at the jump, where it is infinite, so gradient descent gets no useful direction from it. Spiking networks are trained with a surrogate gradient instead. The forward pass keeps the real step, so the network behaves exactly as it will on the chip. Only when the gradient is computed is the step treated as a smooth curve, whose slope tells each weight "this neuron was close to firing, and here is which way to push it".

The stand-in used here is the slope of an arctangent curve. A neuron that came close to firing gets a large gradient, which matches the intuition that a small weight change there would have flipped the spike, and a neuron far from θ gets almost none. The same idea, passing a gradient through a rounding step as if the rounding were not there, is called the straight-through estimator. Training uses it for the chip's integer weights (page G) and for its rounded-down leak.

A.11Signed spikes

An ordinary spiking neuron can only say "yes". With signed spikes, a unit can also fire a negative spike. On the chip such a unit is two neurons. One integrates the input, the other integrates its negative, and whichever crosses its threshold sends a +1 or a −1. A layer can then say "strongly not this" as well as "strongly this", at the cost of twice the neurons and up to four times the connections.

The second neuron's outgoing connections carry negated weights, so the next layer receives +w or −w. Every neuron of a signed unit connects to both neurons of each unit in the next layer, which is why the neurons double and the connections can quadruple, and the chip check (page G) counts them that way. A max pool stays exact with one OR neuron per sign.

A.12Batch normalization

Why this exists

While a network trains, every layer's weights keep changing, so the size of the numbers each layer passes on keeps drifting. The next layer has to keep adjusting to a moving target, and training slows down or stalls. For a spiking neuron the drift is worse. Input that is too weak never reaches the threshold, and input that is too strong fires the neuron on every step. Either way the neuron tells the next layer nothing.

Batch normalization, usually shortened to batch norm, holds each channel's values in a steady range. During training it looks at one channel across the whole batch of examples and works out two numbers, the average of the values and their spread. The spread is the standard deviation, the typical distance of a value from the average. Batch norm subtracts the average and divides by the spread, so the values sit around 0 with a spread of 1. Then it multiplies by a learned scale γ (gamma) and adds a learned shift β (beta), so training can still pick a different range if one works better.

While training, the average and spread come from the current batch, and batch norm keeps a running average of both. After training it uses those running averages, so for each channel it becomes one fixed multiply and one fixed add. That fixed form is what lets it be merged into the weights and thresholds before the network goes on the chip (G.5).

Batch norm was designed for ordinary networks, where a layer runs once per example. A spiking layer runs once per timestep, and plain batch norm works out a separate average and spread for every step. tdBN, threshold-dependent batch normalization (Zheng and coauthors, 2021), is a version made for spiking networks. It works out one average and one spread over all the timesteps of the clip together. It also starts the spread of its output at α times the neuron's threshold θ, so a typical input is about the size of the threshold. The search tries α from 0.5 to 2, and training can still adjust the scale from there.

The dashed line is the threshold θ, and values to its right make a neuron fire. Drag the drift slider to see what training does to a layer with no normalization, then switch normalization on.

With no normalization, a little drift pushes almost every value below θ or above it. Batch norm keeps the values in the same range whatever the drift, but it ignores θ, so a lower threshold makes the neuron fire more often. tdBN follows θ, so the share of values above the threshold stays the same.

A.13Making memorizing harder

Why this exists

A network with many weights can memorize a small dataset, down to details that only belong to the people it trained on. It then does well on those people and worse on anyone new, which is called overfitting (page B). The tools below each make memorizing a little harder, so the network has to rely on what the gestures have in common. Together they are called regularization.

Dropout
On every training step a random set of units is switched off, a different set each step. The network cannot count on any one unit being there, so it learns several ways to reach the same answer. After training, every unit is on. The search tries switching off 10% to 45% of the classifier's inputs, and 5% to 30% of the units between convolution layers.
Weight decay
On every step each weight is pulled a small amount toward zero. A weight stays large only if the training examples keep pushing it there, which makes it harder to build on rare details. One number sets the strength of the pull.
Label smoothing
The usual target says the right gesture has probability 100% and every other gesture 0%. Label smoothing moves a little of that 100% onto the other gestures. With a smoothing of 0.11, the right gesture's target is 90% and each of the other ten gets 1%. The network is no longer rewarded for being completely certain, which tends to make it less overconfident with new people.
Augmentation
Training clips are changed in ways that keep their label, for example shifted a few pixels, so the network never sees exactly the same clip twice (B.7).

A.14Taking better steps

Why this exists

Plain gradient descent (A.3) moves every weight by the same multiple of its gradient. Where the loss forms a long, narrow valley, the steep direction makes it bounce from wall to wall while it crawls along the gentle direction toward the bottom. Real networks are full of valleys like that.

Momentum keeps a running average of recent gradients and steps along that average instead of the latest gradient alone. A direction that stays the same from step to step builds up speed, and bouncing back and forth cancels out.

Adam (Kingma and Ba, 2014) uses momentum and adds a second running average, of each weight's squared gradient. It divides every step by the square root of that average. A weight whose gradients are large and noisy takes smaller steps, and a weight whose gradients are small takes larger ones, so every weight moves at a similar pace. Adam is the most common optimizer in deep learning. AdamW is the same except for weight decay. Adam adds the pull toward zero into the gradient, where the division shrinks it more for some weights than for others. AdamW applies the pull to each weight directly, the same for all of them. The search tries both.

ANIMATION Three optimizers in a narrow valley All three start from the same point and take 40 steps. The shading gets stronger toward the bottom of the valley, which is the dot in the middle.

After 40 steps, plain gradient descent has zigzagged across the valley and is still far from the bottom. Momentum overshoots at first, then speeds along the valley and ends closest. Adam heads for the bottom more directly, because it scales each direction's step separately.

The learning rate itself can change during a run, following a schedule. Most schedules start large, to cover ground quickly, and end small, to settle into the bottom of a valley. Cosine lowers the rate smoothly along a cosine curve, step drops it in a few sudden stages, and one-cycle raises it first and then lowers it. A warm-up starts with a tiny rate for the first few epochs, because large steps from random starting weights can throw training off. D.9 draws all four schedules.

Gradient clipping caps the size of a single step. If one unusual batch produces a huge gradient, the step is shrunk to the cap, so it cannot undo many good steps at once.

A.15Scoring a spiking network

Why this exists

The loss turns a network's answer into one number that training pushes down. A spiking network answers with spikes collected over many steps, so there is a choice about which part of that answer to score.

The rate loss scores what the chip reports. It counts each output neuron's spikes over the whole clip, turns the counts into probabilities with softmax (A.2), and scores them with cross-entropy (A.3). Only the total counts, so the network can point at the wrong gesture for the first half of the clip as long as the total comes out right.

TET, temporal efficient training (Deng and coauthors, 2022), works out the loss at every step from that step's output alone and averages over the steps. Every step is pushed to point at the right gesture, which tends to make training smoother and the final counts more reliable. The chip still answers by counting spikes, so a network trained with TET is scored the same way as any other.

Every spike costs energy on the chip. The firing-rate penalty adds a second term to the loss, the average firing rate of the neurons times a small weight, so training also rewards firing less. A larger weight gives up some accuracy in exchange for less work (A.9).

In short

An artificial neuron is a weighted sum and an activation, and a network is layers of them, trained by gradient descent on a loss. Convolutions reuse small kernels across the picture, stride and pooling shrink the maps, and the head turns maps into class scores. Spiking neurons and spiking convolutions do the same job with spikes over time, firing, resetting, leaking and integrating, and the network's answer is the count of its output spikes. Squeezing numbers into spikes rounds them and costs some accuracy. Training uses a smooth stand-in for the spike's slope. Batch norm keeps each layer's values in a steady range, dropout, weight decay and label smoothing make memorizing harder, momentum and Adam take better steps, and the loss scores either the clip's total spike count or every step.
